Whatever the source, a basement that stays wet for more than a day or two starts to smell and can grow mold, so do not leave it for the weekend. Tell us whether the space is finished, how deep the water is and where it came from.

Seepage, flood or plumbing: three different problems

The first question is where the water came from, because the answer changes the cleanup and the insurance picture. Seepage is slow moisture coming through the floor or walls, often after days of rain. Flooding is a larger volume from outside, such as a storm or a failed pump. A plumbing leak comes from a supply line, water heater or washing machine inside the house.

Seepage shows as dark, damp patches along the wall base, white mineral deposits on block, or water at the joint where floor meets wall. A plumbing leak is local and steady, usually under a pipe or appliance. Floor drains that back up point toward the sewer line, which is a different job covered on our sewage backup page.

Why water gets into basements here

Saturated soil pushes water against a foundation, which is called hydrostatic pressure. Water finds the weakest spot, usually the floor and wall joint, a crack, a pipe penetration or a window well. A high water table, clogged gutters or soil sloping toward the house makes it worse.

Hopkinton sits in a landscape of wetlands. The town's 2004 housing plan says wetlands and wastewater disposal are significant land use constraints in many parts of town. The state describes the Cedar Swamp area of critical environmental concern, about 1,650 acres shared with Westborough, as mostly wetland that stores floodwater for the Sudbury River basin. Those are regional facts, not a promise about any one street.

Unfinished and finished basements are cleaned differently

An unfinished basement is mostly concrete, block and stored items. Cleanup there is pumping, removing wet boxes, and cleaning and drying the slab and walls. Water does less lasting harm, but block walls hold moisture for a long time.

A finished basement has materials that act like sponges. Carpet, padding, drywall and insulation wick water upward and trap it against framing, so the same flood can be a pump-and-dry job in one house and a gut-and-rebuild job in the next.

Basement safety before you go downstairs

Check these first

  • ✓Do not step into standing water if the panel, outlets or a furnace could be energized.
  • ✓Cut power from the panel only from dry footing, and call 911 or the utility for sparks or a gas smell.
  • ✓Wear boots and gloves, and stay out if the water came from a floor drain or toilet, since that may be sewage.
  • ✓Call a plumber for a leaking pipe or water heater, and a gutter or drainage contractor for outside water.

How we clean and dry a basement

Crews pump out standing water with submersible pumps and extraction units, then remove materials that cannot be saved. A moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera show where moisture sits behind walls and under floors. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers dry the space. The industry reference is ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company how its crews measure dryness.

Concrete and block are the slow part. They look dry on top while still holding water inside, so readings are repeated on each visit. For the equipment and timing behind this stage, see our structural drying and dehumidification page.

Humidity after the water is gone

A basement can feel fine and still carry moisture in the air. Keep windows closed during drying and watch for a musty smell, white powder on block or fresh dark spots on drywall. If they appear, call early, because mold needs only moisture and time.

Insurance and basement water

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance says standard homeowners policies do not cover seepage through basement walls, and no endorsement exists for it. Sump pump failure is excluded unless you add an endorsement, and sewer or drain backup needs one too. A sudden and accidental plumbing leak usually falls under the standard policy. Check your policy or ask your insurer and keep a written list of what is removed.

Homes beside a large pond, such as those in the Lake Maspenock neighborhood, get a short local page of their own.

Preventing the next wet basement

  • Extend downspouts several feet away from the foundation and keep gutters clear.
  • Check that soil slopes away from the house rather than toward it.
  • Test the sump pump each season and consider a battery backup for power cuts during storms.
  • Keep floor drains clear and consider a backwater valve, after checking with the sewer authority.
  • Ask the town about permits before finishing a basement.

Outside flood water carries soil and bacteria, so see flood damage restoration for how that is handled. The state-funded 2018 climate report for Milford cites flooding in 2009, 2012, 2013, 2015 and 2018, and the same program's report for Southborough describes small streams above flood stage in the March and April 2010 storms.
