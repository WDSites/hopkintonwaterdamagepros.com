Choosing a Mold Removal Company in Hopkinton, MA

What to ask, what to expect in writing, and what to watch for when hiring mold help in Massachusetts.

By the Hopkinton Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026 · Hopkinton, MA

Choosing a mold removal company in Hopkinton, MA starts with a surprising fact. Massachusetts does not require a license for mold remediation, so there is no state card to check. A mold removal company is a business that removes mold growth and contaminated materials from a building, and its quality shows in its method, not a badge. This guide turns state DPH advice into questions you can ask before you sign anything, plus what the written paperwork should clearly say.

No license does not mean no standards

DPH states that there are no licenses required to conduct mold remediation in Massachusetts. That puts the work of vetting on you. A company with a clear process and a written scope is easy to evaluate. A company that will not explain how it keeps spores out of the rest of your home is telling you something.

Remember the line between two kinds of work. Removing the mold is one job. Rebuilding drywall, trim and flooring afterward is another, and that rebuild work falls under state home improvement rules, which we cover below.

Questions worth asking every company

How will you contain the area?

Look for physical barriers, negative air or air filtration, and a plan to keep dust from drifting into clean rooms.

What exactly are you removing?

You want a list of materials and the rough size of the area, such as drywall to a set height, not a vague promise.

How will you fix the moisture?

Mold returns if the water source stays. Ask who finds it and how drying is confirmed.

Can I see references?

The state guide suggests asking for a statement of qualifications and references from past customers.

How will it be checked at the end?

Ask what condition the work area must be in before the job counts as done, and who verifies it.

What the written scope should say

A good scope reads like a plan, not a sales line. It should name the rooms and surfaces involved, the materials to be removed, how the area will be contained and how debris leaves the house. It should say what happens with moisture, including drying and any plumbing or roof repair that falls outside the mold company's work.

It should also say what is not included. If rebuilding is separate, the paperwork should say so. Hidden extras are the most common reason a bid looks cheaper than it turns out to be.

Things a clear written scope includes

  • ✓Rooms, surfaces and materials listed by name
  • ✓The containment and air filtration approach
  • ✓How moisture will be found, dried and confirmed
  • ✓What is removed and how it is bagged and discarded
  • ✓What rebuilding is included and what is not
  • ✓Start and completion dates and a payment schedule

Industry standards as a reference point

You may see companies mention ANSI/IICRC S520. It is an industry reference for professional mold remediation, and it is a useful vocabulary to ask about. It is not a Massachusetts license or a legal requirement, so treat it as a conversation starter and not a credential. Ask the company how it follows accepted practice, and ask them to explain it in plain terms.

A company that can describe its containment and cleanup steps clearly is more convincing than one that only names a document.

Payment and contract protections

The DPH guide recommends paying in installments rather than all at once, and withholding final payment until the work is verified. Keep to that. Do not pay the full amount up front.

For rebuild work on an owner-occupied one-to-four-unit home, state home improvement rules apply. Contractors must register, residential contracts above the state threshold must be in writing, and the deposit generally cannot exceed the greater of one-third of the price or the cost of special-order materials. Ask any company for its registration number before you sign, and read the contract before you sign it, with no blank spaces.

Testing, upselling and other warning signs

  • Pressure to sign the same day without a written scope
  • A quote that arrives with no site visit and no list of materials
  • Insistence on lab testing as a required first step, when DPH does not recommend routine mold testing as part of water damage work
  • Claims that a spray or fog alone removes the problem, while the moisture source goes unaddressed
  • A request for the whole payment before the job starts
  • No mention of containment

Why drying belongs in the same conversation

A company that removes mold but ignores the cause has done half a job. If a leak, a damp crawl space or a roof entry point is feeding the growth, it needs to be dealt with at the same time. Ask whether the company can dry the structure itself or coordinate with a team that can, because water mitigation and mold remediation are best planned together.
